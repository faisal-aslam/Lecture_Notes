\section{Why Start With Algebra?}

Lattice-based cryptography is, at its core, about doing arithmetic on \emph{vectors} whose coordinates live in a \emph{finite, wraparound number system}, arranged according to strict algebraic rules. Before we can even state what a lattice is, we need precise answers to questions like:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item What does it mean for a set of numbers to have a well-behaved notion of ``add'' and ``multiply''? (\textbf{Groups, Rings, Fields})
  \item What happens when arithmetic ``wraps around,'' like a clock? (\textbf{Modulus})
  \item What does it mean to build every point in a space out of a small number of building-block directions? (\textbf{Vector spaces, Basis})
  \item What happens when we only allow \emph{whole-number} combinations of those building blocks, instead of any real-number combination? (\textbf{Lattice})
\end{itemize}
This lecture answers each of these in order. By the end, the definition of a lattice (Section~8) should feel like the natural, inevitable next step, not an isolated new idea.

\section{Sets and Binary Operations (Quick Refresher)}

A \textbf{set} is just a collection of objects (numbers, vectors, symbols --- anything). A \textbf{binary operation} on a set $S$ is a rule that takes two elements of $S$ and combines them into a third element, e.g., ordinary addition $+$ takes two numbers and produces their sum. We write $a * b$ for a general binary operation.

An operation is \textbf{closed} on $S$ if combining two elements of $S$ always gives another element of $S$ (never something outside $S$). This sounds obvious but fails more often than you'd expect --- see Example~1 below.

\begin{exbox}{Closure Can Fail}
Let $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ (just these five numbers) and let $*$ be ordinary addition. Then $2 * 4 = 6$, but $6 \notin S$. So ordinary addition is \emph{not} closed on $S$. This is exactly the kind of problem modular arithmetic (Section~5) is built to fix.
\end{exbox}

\section{Groups}

\begin{defbox}{Group}
A set $G$ together with a binary operation $*$ is called a \textbf{group}, written $(G, *)$, if all four of the following hold:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=(G\arabic*)]
  \item \textbf{Closure:} for all $a, b \in G$, $a * b \in G$.
  \item \textbf{Associativity:} for all $a,b,c \in G$, $(a*b)*c = a*(b*c)$.
  \item \textbf{Identity:} there exists an element $e \in G$ such that $a * e = e * a = a$ for all $a \in G$.
  \item \textbf{Inverses:} for every $a \in G$, there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ such that $a * a^{-1} = a^{-1} * a = e$.
\end{enumerate}
If, in addition, $a * b = b * a$ for all $a, b \in G$ (the order doesn't matter), the group is called \textbf{abelian} (commutative).
\end{defbox}

\begin{exbox}{Groups You Already Know}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ --- the integers under addition. Identity is $0$; the inverse of $a$ is $-a$. This is an abelian group.
  \item $(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}, \times)$ --- nonzero real numbers under multiplication. Identity is $1$; the inverse of $a$ is $1/a$. Also abelian. (We had to remove $0$ because $0$ has no multiplicative inverse.)
  \item $(\mathbb{Z}, \times)$ --- integers under multiplication --- is \textbf{not} a group: $2$ has no inverse in $\mathbb{Z}$ (we'd need $1/2$, which isn't an integer). This fails axiom (G4).
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}{Groups Hiding in Less Obvious Places: Matrices and Polynomials}
The three examples above were all sets of plain numbers. Groups show up in far less numeric-looking settings too --- the axioms (G1)--(G4) don't care what the elements \emph{are}, only that closure, associativity, identity, and inverses all hold. Four examples worth having in your back pocket, each a little ``fancier'' than the last:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{$3\times 3$ real matrices under addition, $(M_{3\times 3}(\mathbb{R}), +)$, is a group.} Adding two $3\times 3$ matrices gives another $3\times 3$ matrix (closure); matrix addition is associative; the identity is the all-zeros matrix $\mathbf{0}$; the inverse of $A$ is $-A$ (negate every entry). It's even abelian, since $A+B=B+A$ entrywise. Nothing about this is different in spirit from $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ --- just $9$ numbers moving in lockstep instead of $1$.
  \item \textbf{The same set under multiplication is \emph{not} a group.} Try $(M_{3\times 3}(\mathbb{R}), \times)$ (ordinary matrix multiplication): closure holds (multiplying two $3\times3$ matrices gives another $3\times3$ matrix) and multiplication is associative, and there's an identity, $I_3$. But axiom (G4) fails: a \textbf{singular} matrix (one with $\det = 0$, e.g.\ the all-zeros matrix, or $\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$) has no inverse matrix at all --- there is no $B$ with $AB=BA=I_3$. So the full set of $3\times3$ matrices under multiplication is not a group, for exactly the same structural reason $(\mathbb{Z},\times)$ wasn't: some elements simply lack an inverse. (Also worth noting for later: even restricting to the invertible matrices, this group would \emph{not} be abelian in general --- $AB \ne BA$ for most matrix pairs --- unlike every example so far.)
  \item \textbf{Invertible matrices under multiplication, on the other hand, \emph{do} form a group.} Restrict to only the invertible $3\times 3$ matrices, $GL_3(\mathbb{R}) = \{A \in M_{3\times3}(\mathbb{R}) : \det(A)\ne 0\}$. Now every element has an inverse \emph{by construction} (that's what ``invertible'' means), the product of two invertible matrices is again invertible (closure restored), and $I_3$ is still the identity. This is called the \textbf{general linear group}, and it's the standard fix for the multiplication problem above: throw away the elements that were breaking (G4), and check that what's left is still closed.
  \item \textbf{Unitary matrices under multiplication form a group too --- a more specialized cousin of $GL_3$.} A (complex) $n\times n$ matrix $U$ is \textbf{unitary} if $U^*U = I$, where $U^*$ is the conjugate transpose (Lecture 1, Section~7.2's Hermitian inner product remark is exactly the conjugate this definition needs). The set of $n\times n$ unitary matrices, under multiplication, is a group: the product of two unitary matrices is unitary (check: $(U_1U_2)^*(U_1U_2) = U_2^*U_1^*U_1U_2 = U_2^*IU_2=U_2^*U_2=I$), $I$ itself is unitary, and every unitary $U$ has an inverse --- namely $U^{-1}=U^*$, which is itself unitary. This group, $U(n)$, sits \emph{inside} $GL_n(\mathbb{C})$ as a much smaller, highly-structured subgroup (Section~3.1's subgroup idea, one level up): every unitary matrix is invertible, but very few invertible matrices are unitary.
  \item \textbf{Degree-$<n$ polynomials under addition form a group; under multiplication they do not.} Let $P_n = \{a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_{n-1}x^{n-1} : a_i \in \mathbb{R}\}$ (real polynomials of degree strictly less than $n$). Under addition, $(P_n,+)$ is a group exactly like matrices under addition above: closed (adding two such polynomials keeps the degree below $n$), associative, identity is the zero polynomial, and the inverse of $p(x)$ is $-p(x)$. Under multiplication, it immediately fails on \emph{two} separate grounds: closure fails (multiplying two degree-$(n-1)$ polynomials can produce degree up to $2n-2$, which is no longer in $P_n$ at all), and even ignoring that, most individual polynomials have no multiplicative inverse within polynomials (e.g., there is no polynomial $q(x)$ with $x\cdot q(x) = 1$ for every $x$). This is exactly the polynomial-ring preview from Section~4 below: $(P_n,+)$ being a group but $(P_n,\times)$ not being one is precisely why we need the weaker notion of a \textbf{ring} (two operations, only one of which needs full group structure) to describe polynomials properly, rather than expecting both operations to behave like a group.
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Why Cryptography Cares About Groups}
Many classical cryptographic schemes (Diffie--Hellman, ElGamal, elliptic-curve cryptography) are built directly on top of group structure --- the security relies on certain problems (like ``given $g$ and $g^a$, find $a$'') being hard inside a specific group. Lattice-based cryptography instead builds on richer structures (rings and modules, coming next), but the group is always the first layer underneath.
\end{rmkbox}

Is $(\mathbb{Z}_5 \setminus \{0\}, \times \bmod 5) = (\{1,2,3,4\}, \times \bmod 5)$ a group? Check all four axioms directly by building the multiplication table. (You'll need Section~6 for what ``$\bmod 5$'' means if it's new to you --- come back to this after reading that section.)
\end{exercisebox}

\subsection{Subgroups}

There is one more group-related idea we need before we can properly define a lattice: a group living \emph{inside} another group.

\begin{defbox}{Subgroup}
Let $(G, *)$ be a group. A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a \textbf{subgroup} of $G$ if $H$ is itself a group under the same operation $*$. In practice, this is easiest to check with a simple test: $H$ is a subgroup of $G$ if and only if (1) $H$ is nonempty (usually: it contains the identity $e$), (2) $H$ is closed under $*$ (for $a,b \in H$, $a*b \in H$), and (3) $H$ is closed under inverses (for $a \in H$, $a^{-1} \in H$). Associativity is automatic, since it already held for all of $G$.
\end{defbox}

\begin{exbox}{Subgroups You Can Picture}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item For any integer $n$, the set $n\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2n, -n, 0, n, 2n, \ldots\}$ (all multiples of $n$) is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$: it contains $0$, adding two multiples of $n$ gives another multiple of $n$, and $-(kn)$ is also a multiple of $n$. For example, $2\mathbb{Z}$ is ``all even integers.''
  \item The natural numbers $\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ are \textbf{not} a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$: $\mathbb{N}$ is closed under addition, but it fails closure under inverses --- $1 \in \mathbb{N}$, but $-1 \notin \mathbb{N}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{previewbox}{Why This Matters in Two Sections}
Keep the example $n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ in mind. In Section~8, we will define a lattice as a special kind of subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^n$ --- and $n\mathbb{Z}$ is exactly a simple, one-dimensional lattice in disguise: an evenly-spaced, discrete set of points, closed under addition and negation, sitting inside the larger group $\mathbb{Z}$ (or $\mathbb{R}$).
\end{previewbox}

\subsection{Cosets and Quotient Groups: Where $\mathbb{Z}_n$ Really Comes From}

Section~6 will introduce $\mathbb{Z}_n$ informally, as ``remainders with wraparound arithmetic.'' That's the right intuition, but it's worth seeing the fully rigorous construction now, because it is a direct, satisfying payoff of the subgroup idea --- and it is the same construction (``take a group, collapse it by a subgroup'') that shows up again later when we build the ring $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n+1)$ that Kyber and Dilithium actually use.

\begin{rmkbox}{The One-Sentence Idea, Before Any Symbols}
\textbf{A coset is a ``shifted copy'' of a subgroup.} You take every element of $H$ and slide it over by adding a fixed amount $a$. That's all $a+H$ is: $H$, translated --- nothing more exotic than that.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{defbox}{Coset}
Let $H$ be a subgroup of an abelian group $(G,+)$. For $a \in G$, the \textbf{coset} $a + H$ is the set $\{a + h : h \in H\}$ --- ``shift $H$ by $a$.'' Two elements $a,b \in G$ turn out to produce the \emph{same} coset, $a+H = b+H$, exactly when $a - b \in H$. This gives a clean way to sort all of $G$ into non-overlapping ``buckets,'' each one a shifted copy of $H$.
\end{defbox}

\begin{exbox}{Example 1 --- The Simplest Possible Coset: Evens and Odds}
Let $G = \mathbb{Z}$ and $H = 2\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots,-4,-2,0,2,4,\ldots\}$ (the even numbers; check it's a subgroup exactly as in the ``Subgroups You Can Picture'' example: contains $0$, sum of two evens is even, negative of an even is even). Now form cosets by picking different $a$'s:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item $0 + H = \{\ldots,-4,-2,0,2,4,\ldots\}$ --- same set as $H$ itself (the evens)
  \item $1 + H = \{\ldots,-3,-1,1,3,5,\ldots\}$ --- the odds
  \item $2 + H = \{\ldots,-2,0,2,4,6,\ldots\}$ --- same set as $0+H$ again!
  \item $3 + H = \{\ldots,-1,1,3,5,7,\ldots\}$ --- same set as $1+H$ again!
\end{itemize}
No matter which integer you pick as $a$, you only ever land on \textbf{one of two} possible sets: ``the evens'' or ``the odds.'' Every integer belongs to exactly one of these two buckets --- that's the whole idea of cosets, in the simplest case there is.

\textbf{Why did $0+H$ and $2+H$ turn out to be the same set?} This is exactly the rule from the defbox above: $a+H=b+H$ iff $a-b\in H$. Check: $2-0=2$, and $2$ is even, so $2\in H$ --- that's \emph{why} they collapsed to the same coset. Meanwhile $1-0=1$, which is odd, not in $H$ --- so $1+H$ is genuinely a different coset from $0+H$.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}{Example 2 --- A ``Clock'' Version: The Cosets of $5\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Z}$}
Same idea as Example 1, one notch more general: let $H = 5\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots,-10,-5,0,5,10,\ldots\}$. There are exactly \textbf{5} distinct cosets:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Coset} & \textbf{What's in it} & \textbf{``Bucket''} \\
\hline
$0+5\mathbb{Z}$ & $\ldots,-5,0,5,10,\ldots$ & remainder $0$ \\
$1+5\mathbb{Z}$ & $\ldots,-4,1,6,11,\ldots$ & remainder $1$ \\
$2+5\mathbb{Z}$ & $\ldots,-3,2,7,12,\ldots$ & remainder $2$ \\
$3+5\mathbb{Z}$ & $\ldots,-2,3,8,13,\ldots$ & remainder $3$ \\
$4+5\mathbb{Z}$ & $\ldots,-1,4,9,14,\ldots$ & remainder $4$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
You have secretly been computing cosets your whole life: \textbf{a coset of $n\mathbb{Z}$ is just ``everything with the same remainder when divided by $n$.''} Every integer sorts into exactly one bucket, and there are exactly $n$ buckets. This is precisely $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (Section~6), and it's exactly the same pattern as the $3\mathbb{Z}$ case used later in this lecture --- there is nothing special about $5$ here, only that it makes the ``$n$ buckets'' pattern easy to see with more than $2$ or $3$ of them.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}{Example 3 --- A Picture You Can Draw: Lines in the Plane}
This one is worth sitting with, because it connects straight to the lattice tilings of Section~8.5. Let $G = \mathbb{R}^2$ (the plane, under vector addition) and let $H$ be a line through the origin --- say $H = \{(t,t) : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$, the line $y=x$ (check it's a subgroup: contains $(0,0)$; sum of two points on the line is on the line; negation of a point on the line is on the line).

Pick a point $a=(1,0)$, not on the line, and form the coset:
$$
(1,0)+H = \{(1+t,\ t) : t\in\mathbb{R}\}.
$$
This is the line $y=x-1$ --- \textbf{the same line $H$, just shifted to pass through $(1,0)$ instead of the origin.} Pick a different point on that same shifted line, say $a'=(3,2)$: then $a'+H$ is \emph{also} the line $y=x-1$ --- same coset, different name. Check the rule: $a-a' = (1,0)-(3,2) = (-2,-2)$, and $(-2,-2)=(t,t)$ with $t=-2$, so $(-2,-2)\in H$ --- confirming $(1,0)+H = (3,2)+H$.

\textbf{The picture:} the cosets of $H$ are exactly all the lines parallel to $H$. The whole plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ gets sliced into infinitely many parallel lines, and every point of the plane lies on exactly one of them. This ``slicing the whole space into non-overlapping parallel copies'' picture is \emph{exactly} the fundamental-domain tiling picture of Section~8.5 --- cosets and lattice tilings are the same idea, one dimension apart in this example.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}{The Cosets of $3\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ (the Version Used Later in This Lecture)}
Take $G = \mathbb{Z}$ and $H = 3\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots,-3,0,3,6,\ldots\}$ (a subgroup, per the ``Subgroups You Can Picture'' example in Section~3.1). The cosets are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item $0 + 3\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots,-3,0,3,6,\ldots\}$ (multiples of 3)
  \item $1 + 3\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots,-2,1,4,7,\ldots\}$ (remainder $1$ when divided by 3)
  \item $2 + 3\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots,-1,2,5,8,\ldots\}$ (remainder $2$ when divided by 3)
\end{itemize}
Every integer falls into exactly one of these three buckets --- and which bucket it falls into is precisely its remainder mod $3$. There is no fourth distinct coset: $3 + 3\mathbb{Z} = 0 + 3\mathbb{Z}$ (check: $3-0=3 \in 3\mathbb{Z}$), so the pattern repeats.
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{The Two Facts Worth Holding Onto}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Same-coset test:} $a+H = b+H$ if and only if $a-b \in H$ --- this is how you check whether two things you've written down are secretly the same coset (used explicitly in all three examples above).
  \item \textbf{No partial overlap:} two cosets are either \emph{identical} or \emph{completely disjoint} --- never overlapping-but-not-equal. That's what lets cosets cleanly partition $G$ into non-overlapping buckets, with nothing left over and nothing double-counted.
\end{enumerate}
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{defbox}{Quotient Group}
The set of all distinct cosets of $H$ in $G$, with addition defined by $(a+H) + (b+H) := (a+b) + H$, is itself a group --- the \textbf{quotient group}, written $G/H$ (``$G$ mod $H$''). Its identity element is the coset $0 + H = H$ itself.
\end{defbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{$\mathbb{Z}_n$ Is Not Just \emph{Like} $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ --- It \emph{Is} $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$}
Compare the coset example above to Section~6: the three cosets of $3\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ correspond \emph{exactly} to the three elements $\{0,1,2\}$ of $\mathbb{Z}_3$, and coset addition matches addition mod $3$ exactly. This is not a coincidence or an analogy --- it is a theorem: $\mathbb{Z}_n$, as defined informally in Section~6, is precisely the quotient group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. ``Wraparound arithmetic'' and ``sorting integers into cosets of $n\mathbb{Z}$'' are two descriptions of the exact same object. This is the rigorous justification for a claim we made without proof in Section~6: that $\mathbb{Z}_n$ really is a well-defined group (in fact ring) under addition and multiplication with wraparound --- it inherits this structure directly from being a quotient of $\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{exercisebox}{Practice}
Let $H = 4\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$. List all distinct cosets of $H$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, and confirm there are exactly $4$ of them, matching $\mathbb{Z}_4 = \{0,1,2,3\}$. Which coset does $-5$ belong to?
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Rings}

A ring is what you get when you ask a set to support \emph{two} binary operations that play two structurally different roles --- one behaves like addition (fully invertible), the other behaves like multiplication (weaker, no inverses required). We always \emph{label} these two operations ``$+$'' and ``$\times$,'' but --- and this is worth being precise about, since it's easy to misread --- \textbf{the labels do not mean the operations have to literally be school-arithmetic addition and multiplication}. They can be almost any two operations at all, as long as together they satisfy the axioms below. ``$+$'' and ``$\times$'' are names for the \emph{roles} the two operations play, not a requirement about what the operations must concretely do.

\begin{defbox}{Ring}
A set $R$ with two binary operations $+$ and $\times$ is a \textbf{ring} $(R, +, \times)$ if:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=(R\arabic*)]
  \item $(R, +)$ is an abelian group (with identity element usually written $0$).
  \item $\times$ is associative: $(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)$.
  \item The distributive laws hold: $a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$ and $(a+b)\times c = (a \times c) + (b \times c)$.
\end{enumerate}
Note: a ring does \emph{not} need multiplicative inverses, and multiplication need not be commutative in general (though in every ring we use in this thesis, it will be --- these are called \textbf{commutative rings}). If there is an element $1 \in R$ with $a \times 1 = 1 \times a = a$ for all $a$, we say $R$ is a \textbf{ring with unity}.
\end{defbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{``$+$'' and ``$\times$'' Are Role Labels, Not a Restriction to Arithmetic}
Compare this to groups (Section~3): a group has \emph{one} operation, and we wrote it as a generic $*$ precisely to emphasize that it could be anything (addition, multiplication, matrix multiplication, function composition --- whatever satisfies the axioms). A ring needs \emph{two} operations, and it turns out we always call them ``$+$'' and ``$\times$'' --- but this is a naming convention for the two \emph{roles}, not a claim that the operations must be ordinary numeric addition and multiplication. Concretely:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{$n \times n$ matrices:} ``$+$'' is ordinary matrix addition (this forms an abelian group), and ``$\times$'' is matrix \emph{multiplication} (associative, distributes over $+$) --- but matrix multiplication is \textbf{not} commutative in general, which is fine, since a ring's axioms above don't require it (only \emph{commutative} rings require it, and this one wouldn't qualify as one).
  \item \textbf{Boolean rings} (used in digital logic): ``$+$'' is XOR and ``$\times$'' is AND on $\{0,1\}$ --- nothing to do with school arithmetic at all, yet every ring axiom above holds.
\end{itemize}
So whenever you meet a new ring in this course, the first question to ask is not ``is $+$ really addition and is $\times$ really multiplication?'' but rather: \emph{what are the two operations here, and do they satisfy axioms (R1)--(R3)?} The labels ``$+$''/``$\times$'' just tell you which operation is playing which role once you've checked that.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{exbox}{Rings You Already Know}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item $(\mathbb{Z}, +, \times)$ --- the integers form a commutative ring with unity $1$. Notice $2$ still has no multiplicative inverse, and that's fine --- rings don't require one.
  \item \textbf{Polynomial rings}, e.g. $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the set of all polynomials with integer coefficients (like $3x^2 - x + 7$), with ordinary polynomial addition and multiplication. This is a ring for exactly the same reason $\mathbb{Z}$ is.
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{previewbox}{The Ring That Kyber and Dilithium Actually Use}
Both Kyber and Dilithium do their arithmetic inside a specific ring of the form
$$R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x] / (x^n + 1),$$
read as: ``polynomials with coefficients taken modulo $q$ (Section~6), where we also reduce powers of $x$ using the rule $x^n \equiv -1$.'' This looks intimidating right now, but it is built from exactly the pieces in this lecture: a polynomial ring (this section), with coefficients from $\mathbb{Z}_q$ (Section~6), further ``folded'' by a fixed rule. We will unpack this fully in a later lecture --- for now, just notice that ``ring'' is not an abstract curiosity; it's the literal object the cryptography is built on.
\end{previewbox}

\subsection{Zero Divisors: A Structural Reason Some Rings Aren't Fields}

We will soon see (Section~5) that $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is a field exactly when $n$ is prime. Section~6.1 will justify this computationally, via $\gcd$. Here is the deeper, structural reason \emph{why} --- worth seeing now, while we're still inside general ring theory.

\begin{defbox}{Zero Divisor}
In a ring $R$, a nonzero element $a$ is a \textbf{zero divisor} if there exists some \emph{nonzero} $b \in R$ with $a \times b = 0$. A commutative ring with unity that has \emph{no} zero divisors (other than needing $a$ or $b$ to be $0$) is called an \textbf{integral domain}.
\end{defbox}

\begin{exbox}{Zero Divisors in $\mathbb{Z}_6$, Absent in $\mathbb{Z}_5$}
In $\mathbb{Z}_6$: $2 \times 3 = 6 \equiv 0 \pmod 6$, but neither $2$ nor $3$ is $0$. So $2$ and $3$ are both zero divisors --- $\mathbb{Z}_6$ is \emph{not} an integral domain. Contrast this with $\mathbb{Z}_5$: checking every pair of nonzero elements ($1,2,3,4$), no product is ever $\equiv 0 \pmod 5$ (e.g., $2\times 3=6\equiv 1$, $4\times 4 = 16 \equiv 1$, etc.) --- $\mathbb{Z}_5$ has no zero divisors.
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Why This Explains the Prime/Composite Split}
\textbf{Fact:} every field is automatically an integral domain (if $a \ne 0$ and $ab=0$, multiply both sides by $a^{-1}$ to get $b=0$ --- so no nonzero $b$ can pair with a nonzero $a$ to give $0$). Contrapositive: \emph{if a ring has a zero divisor, it cannot be a field.} Now, $\mathbb{Z}_n$ has a zero divisor exactly when $n$ is composite: write $n = jk$ with $1 < j,k < n$; then $j \times k = n \equiv 0 \pmod n$, with neither $j$ nor $k$ equal to $0$ in $\mathbb{Z}_n$. So every composite modulus produces zero divisors, which immediately rules out being a field --- a purely structural explanation, with no $\gcd$ computation needed, of the same fact Section~6.1 reaches computationally.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{exercisebox}{Practice}

\end{exercisebox}

\section{Fields}

\begin{defbox}{Field}
A \textbf{field} is a set $F$ with two binary operations $+$ and $\times$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=(F\arabic*)]
  \item $(F, +)$ is an abelian group, over \emph{all} of $F$ (identity element $0$).
  \item $(F \setminus \{0\}, \times)$ is an abelian group, over $F$ \textbf{with $0$ removed} (identity element $1$).
  \item The distributive law holds: $a \times (b+c) = (a\times b) + (a \times c)$.
\end{enumerate}
Informally: a field is a set where you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide (by anything except $0$) and always stay inside the set. In short --- \textbf{a field is two abelian groups sharing one set, glued together by distributivity.}
\end{defbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Why $0$ Has to Be Excluded, and It's Not Optional}
In \emph{any} structure with a distributive law, $0 \times x = 0$ for every $x$ (this follows from distributivity itself, not from an extra rule: $0 \times x = (0+0)\times x = 0\times x + 0 \times x$, so subtracting $0\times x$ from both sides gives $0 = 0 \times x$). So $0$ could only have a multiplicative inverse if $0 \times x = 1$ for some $x$ --- but the left side is always $0$, never $1$ (as long as $1 \ne 0$, which we always assume). So $0$ is permanently excluded from having an inverse, in every field, without exception; it isn't a special case someone chose, it's forced by the arithmetic.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{defbox}{Subtraction and Division Are \emph{Derived}, Not Extra}
Neither subtraction nor division is a new, independent operation requiring its own axiom --- both are just notation for ``combine with an inverse,'' one level for each operation:
$$
a - b := a + (-b), \qquad \frac{a}{b} := a \times b^{-1} \ \ (b \ne 0),
$$
where $-b$ is guaranteed to exist by $(F,+)$ being a group (F1), and $b^{-1}$ is guaranteed to exist, for every \emph{nonzero} $b$, by $(F\setminus\{0\},\times)$ being a group (F2). So ``a field supports division'' is not an extra requirement on top of the definition --- it falls straight out of (F2) once every nonzero element is already promised an inverse. This is exactly why (F2) excludes $0$: since $0$ can never have an inverse (see the remark above), ``dividing by $0$'' can never be defined this way, no matter what field you're in.
\end{defbox}

\begin{exbox}{Division in $\mathbb{Z}_5$, Worked Out}
What is $4 \div 3$ in $\mathbb{Z}_5$? First find $3^{-1} \bmod 5$: $3 \times 2 = 6 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$, so $3^{-1} = 2$. Then $4 \div 3 := 4 \times 3^{-1} = 4 \times 2 = 8 \equiv 3 \pmod 5$. Check: $3 \times 3 = 9 \equiv 4 \pmod 5$ --- correct, exactly as ``$4 \div 3 = 3$'' should verify.
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{The One-Line Takeaway}
\textbf{Subtraction is the additive inverse; division is the multiplicative inverse.} Same trick, applied once per operation --- $a-b$ means ``add $a$ and $b$'s additive inverse,'' $a/b$ means ``multiply $a$ by $b$'s multiplicative inverse.'' The one asymmetry between them traces directly back to (F1) vs.\ (F2): \emph{every} element of $F$ has an additive inverse, since $(F,+)$ is a group over \emph{all} of $F$ --- so subtraction always works. But \emph{only nonzero} elements have a multiplicative inverse, since $(F\setminus\{0\},\times)$ is a group only after removing $0$ --- so division by $b$ only ever works when $b \ne 0$.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{factbox}{Every Field Is a Ring}
Every field $F$ (Definition above) is automatically a commutative ring with unity --- in fact, an \textbf{integral domain} (Section~4.1). \emph{Why:} (F1) is exactly ring axiom (R1). Associativity and distributivity of $\times$ (ring axioms (R2)--(R3)) are inherited directly from $\times$ being a group operation on $F \setminus \{0\}$ together with (F3). So a field satisfies every ring axiom, plus two extra guarantees stacked on top: $\times$ is commutative, and every \emph{nonzero} element is invertible. This is why some textbooks instead \emph{define} a field as ``a commutative ring with unity in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse'' --- that phrasing and the definition above pick out exactly the same objects; it's just built in the reverse order (starting from ``ring'' and adding a condition, instead of starting from two groups and gluing them together). Finally, a field is automatically an integral domain too: if $ab=0$ with $a \ne 0$, multiplying both sides by $a^{-1}$ forces $b = a^{-1}(ab) = a^{-1}\cdot 0 = 0$, so there can be no zero divisors.
\end{factbox}

\begin{exbox}{Fields and Non-Fields}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item $\mathbb{Q}$ (rationals), $\mathbb{R}$ (reals), $\mathbb{C}$ (complex numbers) are all fields --- you can divide by any nonzero element and stay inside the set.
  \item $\mathbb{Z}$ is \textbf{not} a field: $2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ has no multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{Z}$ (again, $1/2 \notin \mathbb{Z}$). $\mathbb{Z}$ is a ring, but not a field.
  \item $\mathbb{Z}_5 = \{0,1,2,3,4\}$ under addition and multiplication mod $5$ \emph{is} a field --- every nonzero element has an inverse (you checked this, or will check this, in the Section~3 subgroup/group exercise). Fields built this way, from $\mathbb{Z}_p$ with $p$ prime, are called \textbf{finite fields} or \textbf{Galois fields}, written $\mathbb{F}_p$ or $GF(p)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{The One-Sentence Summary So Far}
Group $\subset$ Ring $\subset$ Field, in the sense that a field is a ring with extra structure (division), and a ring is built from a group with extra structure (a second operation). Each step adds a capability: groups let you undo one operation (inverses); rings add a second, distributive operation; fields guarantee \emph{both} operations are fully invertible (except division by $0$).
\end{rmkbox}

\section{The Modulus: Arithmetic That Wraps Around}

\begin{defbox}{Congruence Modulo $n$}
For a fixed positive integer $n$ (the \textbf{modulus}), two integers $a$ and $b$ are \textbf{congruent modulo $n$}, written
$$a \equiv b \pmod{n},$$
if $n$ divides $(a-b)$ evenly --- equivalently, $a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder when divided by $n$. The set of possible remainders, $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots, n-1\}$, together with addition and multiplication performed ``with wraparound,'' is written $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (or $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$).
\end{defbox}

\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.1]
  \foreach \i in {0,...,11} {
    \node[circle, draw, minimum size=0.75cm, font=\small] at ({90-\i*30}:2) {\i};
  }

  \node[blue, font=\small] at (3.2,1.9) {$9 + 5 = 14 \equiv 2 \pmod{12}$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{The clock analogy for modular arithmetic: $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ works exactly like a 12-hour clock face. $9$ o'clock plus $5$ hours wraps around to $2$ o'clock, i.e., $9+5=14 \equiv 2 \pmod{12}$.}
\label{fig:clock}
\end{figure}

\begin{exbox}{Computing Modulo}
$17 \bmod 5$: divide $17$ by $5$ to get $3$ remainder $2$, so $17 \equiv 2 \pmod 5$. Similarly, $23 \equiv 3 \pmod{10}$, and $-1 \equiv n-1 \pmod n$ (e.g., $-1 \equiv 4 \pmod 5$) --- negative numbers wrap around too.
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{When Is $\mathbb{Z}_n$ a Ring? When Is It a Field?}
$\mathbb{Z}_n$ is \emph{always} a commutative ring with unity, for any $n \geq 2$. But it is a \emph{field} only when $n = p$ is \textbf{prime}. If $n$ is composite (e.g., $n=6$), some nonzero elements fail to have multiplicative inverses --- e.g., in $\mathbb{Z}_6$, there is no $x$ with $2x \equiv 1 \pmod 6$, since $2$ shares a common factor with $6$. This is exactly why cryptographic schemes that need a field (rather than just a ring) are built with a prime modulus $q$. (Section~4.1 gave the deeper structural reason: composite $n$ always produces zero divisors, and a ring with zero divisors can never be a field.)
\end{rmkbox}

\subsection{Finding Inverses Without Guessing: The Extended Euclidean Algorithm}

So far, the only way we've found a multiplicative inverse mod $n$ is trial and error (Exercise: ``find $x$ with $3x \equiv 1 \pmod 7$'' --- you could just try $x=1,2,3,\ldots$ until one works). That's fine for small numbers, but real cryptographic moduli are enormous (Kyber's modulus is $q=3329$; RSA moduli have hundreds of digits) --- trial and error is completely impractical. We need an actual algorithm.

\begin{defbox}{Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)}
For integers $a, b$ (not both zero), $\gcd(a,b)$ is the largest positive integer that divides both $a$ and $b$. Two integers are \textbf{coprime} (or relatively prime) if $\gcd(a,b) = 1$.
\end{defbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Fact: When Does an Inverse Exist?}
An element $a \in \mathbb{Z}_n$ has a multiplicative inverse if and only if $\gcd(a,n) = 1$. This is why every nonzero element of $\mathbb{Z}_p$ (for prime $p$) has an inverse: any $a$ with $1 \le a \le p-1$ automatically satisfies $\gcd(a,p)=1$, since $p$'s only divisors are $1$ and $p$ itself.
\end{rmkbox}

The classical \textbf{Euclidean Algorithm} computes $\gcd(a,b)$ by repeated division with remainder: $\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$, stopping when the remainder hits $0$. The \textbf{Extended} Euclidean Algorithm does this same process but also tracks, at \emph{every single step}, how the current remainder can be written as a combination $a\cdot x + b\cdot y$ (integers $x,y$) --- and when the algorithm finishes with $\gcd(a,b)=1$, the row where the remainder equals $1$ hands you the inverse directly. The cleanest way to track this --- much easier to follow than plain back-substitution --- is a running \textbf{table} with one row per step.

\begin{defbox}{The Extended Euclidean Algorithm, as a Table}
To find $\gcd(a,b)$ together with integers $x,y$ satisfying $a\cdot x + b\cdot y = \gcd(a,b)$, build a table with columns $q$ (quotient), $r$ (remainder), $x$, $y$, starting with two fixed initial rows and then one new row per division step:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c}
step & $q$ & $r$ & $x$ & $y$ \\
\hline
$-1$ & --- & $a$ & $1$ & $0$ \\
$0$ & --- & $b$ & $0$ & $1$ \\
$1$ & $q_1 = \lfloor r_{-1}/r_0 \rfloor$ & $r_1 = r_{-1} - q_1 r_0$ & $x_1 = x_{-1} - q_1 x_0$ & $y_1 = y_{-1} - q_1 y_0$ \\
$2$ & $q_2 = \lfloor r_0/r_1 \rfloor$ & $r_2 = r_0 - q_2 r_1$ & $x_2 = x_0 - q_2 x_1$ & $y_2 = y_0 - q_2 y_1$ \\
$\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Every single row satisfies the \textbf{invariant} $r_i = a \cdot x_i + b \cdot y_i$ by construction --- this is the whole mechanism, and it's worth checking explicitly on at least one row the first time you use this table, to convince yourself it isn't magic. Stop once a row produces $r_i = 0$; the \emph{previous} row's $r$ is $\gcd(a,b)$, and that row's $x,y$ are exactly the combination you want.
\end{defbox}

\begin{exbox}{Table Method, Short Version: $\gcd(7,3)$ and $3^{-1} \bmod 7$}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c|l}
step & $q$ & $r$ & $x$ & $y$ & check: $7x+3y$ \\
\hline
$-1$ & --- & $7$ & $1$ & $0$ & $7(1)+3(0)=7$ \checkmark \\
$0$ & --- & $3$ & $0$ & $1$ & $7(0)+3(1)=3$ \checkmark \\
$1$ & $\lfloor 7/3\rfloor=2$ & $7-2(3)=1$ & $1-2(0)=1$ & $0-2(1)=-2$ & $7(1)+3(-2)=1$ \checkmark \\
$2$ & $\lfloor 3/1\rfloor=3$ & $3-3(1)=0$ & $0-3(1)=-3$ & $1-3(-2)=7$ & (stop: $r=0$) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The row \emph{before} $r$ hit $0$ is step $1$: $r_1 = 1 = \gcd(7,3)$, with $x_1=1, y_1=-2$. So $7(1) + 3(-2) = 1$, i.e., $3 \times (-2) \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, giving $3^{-1} \equiv -2 \equiv 5 \pmod 7$ --- matching what trial and error would also find, but now via a method with a clear, mechanical stopping point.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}{Table Method, Longer Version: $15^{-1} \bmod 26$}
Here the table earns its keep --- more steps than the tiny example above, but the process is identical, row by row.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c}
step & $q$ & $r$ & $x$ & $y$ \\
\hline
$-1$ & --- & $26$ & $1$ & $0$ \\
$0$ & --- & $15$ & $0$ & $1$ \\
$1$ & $\lfloor 26/15\rfloor=1$ & $26-1(15)=11$ & $1-1(0)=1$ & $0-1(1)=-1$ \\
$2$ & $\lfloor 15/11\rfloor=1$ & $15-1(11)=4$ & $0-1(1)=-1$ & $1-1(-1)=2$ \\
$3$ & $\lfloor 11/4\rfloor=2$ & $11-2(4)=3$ & $1-2(-1)=3$ & $-1-2(2)=-5$ \\
$4$ & $\lfloor 4/3\rfloor=1$ & $4-1(3)=1$ & $-1-1(3)=-4$ & $2-1(-5)=7$ \\
$5$ & $\lfloor 3/1\rfloor=3$ & $3-3(1)=0$ & $3-3(-4)=15$ & $-5-3(7)=-26$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The row before $r$ hit $0$ is step $4$: $r_4 = 1 = \gcd(26,15)$, with $x_4=-4, y_4=7$. \textbf{Check the invariant directly:} $26(-4) + 15(7) = -104 + 105 = 1$ \checkmark. Reading this modulo $26$: $15 \times 7 \equiv 1 \pmod{26}$, so $15^{-1} \equiv 7 \pmod{26}$. \textbf{Final check:} $15 \times 7 = 105 = 4\times26+1 \equiv 1 \pmod{26}$. Correct.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exercisebox}{Practice}
Use the table method above (not back-substitution, and not trial and error) to find $5^{-1} \bmod 11$. Build the full table --- steps $-1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ --- and stop at the correct row. Then check your answer by direct multiplication.
\end{exercisebox}

\begin{previewbox}{Why Cryptography Loves the Modulus}
Modular arithmetic gives us two things cryptography needs badly: (1) a \textbf{finite} number system (so keys and computations have a fixed, bounded size, no matter how much you compute), and (2) built-in \textbf{one-wayness} in places --- e.g., given only $a \bmod n$, you cannot tell which of infinitely many integers $a$ actually was. The Learning-With-Errors (LWE) problem from the proposal document hides its secret inside equations that live in exactly this kind of finite, modular number system (there, the modulus is usually called $q$).
\end{previewbox}

(a) Compute $23 \times 15 \bmod 7$. (b) List all elements of $\mathbb{Z}_8$ that \emph{do not} have a multiplicative inverse, and explain why (hint: compare each element to $8$). (c) Confirm $\mathbb{Z}_7$ is a field by checking that $3$ has a multiplicative inverse mod $7$ (find $x$ such that $3x \equiv 1 \pmod 7$).
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Vector Spaces, Basis, and Dimension}

\begin{defbox}{Vector Space}
A \textbf{vector space} over a field $F$ is a set $V$ (whose elements are called \textbf{vectors}) equipped with an addition operation $V \times V \to V$ and a scalar multiplication operation $F \times V \to V$, satisfying, for all $u,v,w \in V$ and all $a,b \in F$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=(V\arabic*)]
  \item \textbf{Closure of addition:} $u+v \in V$.
  \item \textbf{Associativity of addition:} $(u+v)+w = u+(v+w)$.
  \item \textbf{Commutativity of addition:} $u+v = v+u$.
  \item \textbf{Additive identity:} there exists $\vec 0 \in V$ with $v + \vec 0 = v$ for all $v$.
  \item \textbf{Additive inverses:} for every $v \in V$, there exists $-v \in V$ with $v+(-v) = \vec 0$.
  \item \textbf{Compatibility of scalar multiplication with field multiplication:} $a(bv) = (ab)v$.
  \item \textbf{Identity element of scalar multiplication:} $1v = v$, where $1$ is the multiplicative identity of $F$.
  \item \textbf{Distributivity, two directions:} $a(u+v) = au+av$ \emph{and} $(a+b)v = av+bv$.
\end{enumerate}
Axioms (V1)--(V5) say exactly ``$(V,+)$ is an abelian group'' (Section~3) --- nothing new there. Axioms (V6)--(V8) are the genuinely new content: they say scalar multiplication by field elements behaves consistently with the field's own addition and multiplication (Sections~4--5). The most important example for us: $\mathbb{R}^n$, the set of all $n$-tuples of real numbers, is a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$ --- and it's a good exercise to mentally check each of (V1)--(V8) holds there before moving on.
\end{defbox}

\begin{defbox}{Linear Combination, Span, Linear Independence}
Given vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_k \in V$ and scalars $c_1, \ldots, c_k \in F$, the vector $c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \cdots + c_k v_k$ is called a \textbf{linear combination} of $v_1, \ldots, v_k$. The set of \emph{all} linear combinations of a set of vectors is called their \textbf{span}. The vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_k$ are \textbf{linearly independent} if the only way to write $0 = c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_k v_k$ is with every $c_i = 0$ --- informally, no vector in the set is ``redundant'' (expressible using the others).
\end{defbox}

\subsection{Testing Linear Independence: Row Operations and Row Echelon Form}

The definition above is a yes/no test stated abstractly in terms of the $c_i$'s. For concrete vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, it is also a completely mechanical computation --- and the standard tool for carrying it out, \textbf{Gaussian elimination}, is worth setting up properly here, since we will lean on it again once bases and lattices enter the picture.

\begin{defbox}{Elementary Row Operations}
Given a matrix, the following three operations on its rows are called \textbf{elementary row operations}:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=(E\arabic*)]
  \item \textbf{Row swap:} interchange two rows, $R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$.
  \item \textbf{Row scaling:} multiply a row by a nonzero scalar, $R_i \to c\,R_i$ for some $c \neq 0$.
  \item \textbf{Row addition (replacement):} add a scalar multiple of one row to another, $R_i \to R_i + c\,R_j$ (for $i \neq j$).
\end{enumerate}
Each operation is \textbf{reversible} by another operation of the same type: a swap undoes itself; scaling by $c$ is undone by scaling by $1/c$; adding $c R_j$ is undone by adding $-c R_j$. This reversibility is exactly why row operations never destroy information --- every row of the new matrix is some combination of the old rows, and (because the operation can be undone) every old row is likewise recoverable as a combination of the new ones.
\end{defbox}

\begin{factbox}{Row Operations Preserve the Span --- and Hence Independence --- of the Rows}
Let $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ be the rows of a matrix $A$. Applying any sequence of elementary row operations produces a new matrix $A'$ whose rows $v_1',\ldots,v_k'$ \textbf{span exactly the same space} as $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ did --- because, by the remark above, each new row is some linear combination of the old rows, and each old row is recoverable as a combination of the new ones. In particular: $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ are linearly independent \textbf{if and only if} $v_1',\ldots,v_k'$ are. Row operations are therefore ``free'' for this purpose --- we can simplify a set of vectors as aggressively as we like without ever changing the yes/no answer to the independence question, exactly the same way row operations leave $\gcd$, solution sets, and (as we'll see in Section~8.5) a lattice's determinant unchanged under the right kind of transformation.
\end{factbox}

\begin{defbox}{Row Echelon Form}
A matrix is in \textbf{row echelon form (REF)} if:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=(\arabic*)]
  \item Every all-zero row (if any) lies below every nonzero row.
  \item In each nonzero row, its first nonzero entry --- the \textbf{leading entry} or \textbf{pivot} --- lies strictly to the right of the leading entry of the row above it (so the pivots form a descending ``staircase'').
\end{enumerate}
Any matrix can be brought to row echelon form using finitely many elementary row operations --- systematically eliminating entries below each pivot, top to bottom, is exactly \textbf{Gaussian elimination}. The number of nonzero rows in the resulting echelon form is called the \textbf{rank} of the matrix; by the Fact above, this number doesn't depend on which particular sequence of row operations you used to get there.
\end{defbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Turning This Into an Independence Test}
Stack $v_1,\ldots,v_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ as the $k$ rows of a $k \times n$ matrix $A$, and row-reduce $A$ to echelon form $A'$. Since row operations never change independence, $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ are linearly independent \textbf{exactly when $A'$ has no zero row} --- equivalently, when $\mathrm{rank}(A) = k$ (every row produced its own pivot). If instead a zero row appears, the original vectors are dependent --- and the specific combination of row operations that produced that zero row \emph{is} a witness: it records exactly which combination of the original rows sums to $\vec 0$. The worked example below shows a clean way to read that combination off directly.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{exbox}{Row Reduction in Action: Testing $v_1=(1,2,3)$, $v_2=(2,3,4)$, $v_3=(1,1,1)$}
Stack the vectors as rows, and --- to make the eventual dependency relation easy to read off --- augment the matrix on the right with the $3\times 3$ identity, one column per original vector:
$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc}
1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
2 & 3 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
1 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right]
\begin{array}{l} \leftarrow v_1 \\ \leftarrow v_2 \\ \leftarrow v_3 \end{array}
$$
The idea: whatever combination of rows we apply to the left block, we apply \emph{identically} to the right block --- so the right block always records ``this row currently equals (this combination) of $v_1,v_2,v_3$.''

\textbf{Step 1: eliminate below the first pivot.} Use $R_1$ (pivot in column 1) to clear column 1 in $R_2$ and $R_3$:
$$
R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1, \qquad R_3 \to R_3 - R_1.
$$
$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc}
1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & -1 & -2 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & -1 & -2 & -1 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right]
$$

\textbf{Step 2: eliminate below the second pivot.} $R_2$'s leading entry is now in column 2; use it to clear column 2 in $R_3$:
$$
R_3 \to R_3 - R_2.
$$
$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc}
1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & -1 & -2 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & -1 & 1
\end{array}\right]
$$
This is row echelon form: two pivots (row 1 in column 1, row 2 in column 2), and one zero row --- so $\mathrm{rank}=2 < 3 = k$, meaning $v_1,v_2,v_3$ \textbf{are linearly dependent}.

\textbf{Reading off the dependency.} Row 3's right-hand block, $(1,-1,1)$, records exactly which combination of the \emph{original} rows produced that zero row: $1\cdot v_1 + (-1)\cdot v_2 + 1\cdot v_3$. Check directly: $v_1 - v_2 + v_3 = (1,2,3)-(2,3,4)+(1,1,1) = (0,0,0)$ \checkmark. So $c_1=1,\ c_2=-1,\ c_3=1$ is a non-trivial solution --- equivalently, $v_2 = v_1+v_3$, which you can also confirm directly.
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Why More Vectors Than Dimensions Forces Dependence}
If $k>n$ (more vectors than the ambient dimension), the vectors are \emph{automatically} dependent --- no computation needed. Setting up $c_1v_1+\cdots+c_kv_k=\vec 0$ gives $n$ equations (one per coordinate) in $k>n$ unknowns $c_1,\ldots,c_k$; row-reducing the coefficient matrix can produce at most $n$ pivots (one per row), so at least $k-n$ of the unknowns are left as \textbf{free variables} --- and a homogeneous system with a free variable always has a nontrivial solution (just set the free variable to $1$ and solve for the rest). We will meet this exact fact again, in geometric disguise, once we reach lattices in Section~8.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{defbox}{Basis and Dimension}
A set of vectors $B = \{b_1, \ldots, b_k\}$ is a \textbf{basis} of a vector space $V$ if (1) $B$ is linearly independent, and (2) $B$ \textbf{spans} $V$ (every vector in $V$ can be written as a linear combination of $B$). Every basis of a given vector space has the same number of elements $k$, called the \textbf{dimension} of $V$.
\end{defbox}

\begin{exbox}{Basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$}
The vectors $e_1 = (1,0)$ and $e_2 = (0,1)$ form the ``standard'' basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$: any vector $(a,b)$ is exactly $a \cdot e_1 + b \cdot e_2$. But this is far from the \emph{only} basis --- $\{(1,1), (1,-1)\}$ is also a valid basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ (check: they're linearly independent, and any $(a,b)$ can be written as a combination of them). A vector space almost always has infinitely many possible bases; some are more convenient to work with than others. \textbf{Keep this fact in mind --- it becomes very important in Section~8.}
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{A Quick Shortcut When $k=n$}
When there are exactly as many vectors as dimensions, there's a fast special-case test that avoids row reduction entirely: stack $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ as the columns (or rows) of a square matrix $A$ and compute $\det(A)$. Nonzero determinant $\Rightarrow$ independent; zero determinant $\Rightarrow$ dependent. This is a quick yes/no check, but --- unlike the row-reduction method above --- it doesn't by itself hand you the explicit nontrivial combination when the answer is ``dependent''; for that, fall back on row reduction (optionally with the identity-augmentation trick above).
\end{rmkbox}

\subsection{Inner Products, Norms, and Orthogonality}

To make ``good basis'' (short, nearly-perpendicular vectors) versus ``bad basis'' (long, nearly-parallel vectors) precise rather than just intuitive, we need a way to measure length and angle.

\begin{defbox}{Inner Product and Norm}
For $x = (x_1,\ldots,x_n), y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the (standard) \textbf{inner product} is $\langle x, y \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i$. The \textbf{norm} (length) of $x$ is $\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x,x \rangle}$. Two vectors are \textbf{orthogonal} if $\langle x,y \rangle = 0$ (the angle between them is $90^\circ$). A basis $\{b_1,\ldots,b_k\}$ is an \textbf{orthogonal basis} if every pair $b_i, b_j$ ($i \ne j$) is orthogonal.
\end{defbox}

\begin{exbox}{Computing Norms and Checking Orthogonality}
For $x=(3,4)$: $\|x\| = \sqrt{3^2+4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$. For $x=(1,1)$ and $y=(1,-1)$: $\langle x,y\rangle = (1)(1)+(1)(-1) = 0$, so these two vectors (the alternate basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ from the previous example) are orthogonal --- a nicer basis, geometrically, than it might have looked at first glance.
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Extending the Inner Product to Complex Vectors}
The formula $\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_i x_iy_i$ above is only correct for \emph{real} vectors, $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^n$. It breaks down over $\mathbb{C}^n$: take a single complex coordinate, $x=(i)$. The formula gives $\langle x,x\rangle = i\cdot i = i^2 = -1$, so $\|x\| = \sqrt{-1}$ --- not a real number at all, let alone a sensible ``length.'' A norm must be a non-negative real number, so this naive formula simply stops being a norm once complex entries are allowed.

The standard fix is to place a \textbf{complex conjugate} on one argument,
$$
\langle x,y\rangle \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\,\overline{y_i} \qquad \text{(the \emph{Hermitian} inner product)},
$$
where $\overline{a+bi} = a-bi$. Now $\langle x,x\rangle = \sum_i x_i\overline{x_i} = \sum_i |x_i|^2$ --- a sum of non-negative real numbers, always real and always $\ge 0$, exactly what a norm needs. And whenever every entry happens to be real, $\overline{y_i}=y_i$, so this formula collapses back \emph{exactly} to the plain dot product in the defbox above --- the conjugate only ever does work when the entries are genuinely complex, and is invisible (a no-op) on real vectors. This is why the definition above was stated over $\mathbb{R}^n$ specifically: every lattice in Lectures~1--4 lives in $\mathbb{R}^n$ or $\mathbb{Z}^n$, so the plain real formula is all we need for now --- the moment complex vectors enter the picture, however, conjugating becomes required, not optional.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{previewbox}{Where Complex Vectors Actually Show Up (Preview of Falcon, Lecture 4)}
This isn't a hypothetical fix-it-just-in-case. Falcon's signing step (``fast Fourier sampling,'' Lecture 4) embeds a ring element of $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n+1)$ into $\mathbb{C}^n$ using the $n$ complex roots of $x^n+1$ --- the \textbf{canonical embedding} --- because in that embedding, the otherwise-awkward polynomial multiplication in $R_q$ becomes simple coordinate-wise multiplication of complex numbers, which is exactly what makes fast Fourier sampling fast. Every norm and inner product computed inside that embedding \emph{is} the Hermitian version defined above, not the plain real dot product. Keep this box in mind when Falcon's sampler resurfaces in Session 5: part of why its floating-point implementation is so delicate to secure is that it's performing real, finite-precision arithmetic on quantities that are conceptually complex-number computations underneath.
\end{previewbox}

\begin{previewbox}{Quantifying ``Good'' vs.\ ``Bad'' Bases}
With norms in hand, we can finally give a real, measurable meaning to the ``good basis / bad basis'' idea flagged repeatedly in this lecture: a good basis has vectors with small norm $\|b_i\|$ that are close to orthogonal; a bad basis for the \emph{same} lattice can have arbitrarily large norms and near-parallel vectors, despite generating an identical set of points. (Here $L$ denotes the \textbf{lattice} generated by the basis $\{b_1,\ldots,b_n\}$ --- all integer combinations of the $b_i$'s, i.e.\ the grid of points they produce; we've been using this good-basis/bad-basis idea informally for a few pages now, and Section~8 below is where $L$ finally gets a full, formal definition. For now, just read $L$ as ``the lattice this basis describes.'') One common numerical measure of ``how good'' a basis is is its \textbf{orthogonality defect}, $\dfrac{\prod_i \|b_i\|}{\det(L)}$, where $\det(L)$ is a single positive number --- the lattice's fundamental-cell volume --- that depends only on $L$ itself, not on which basis is used to describe it (defined precisely in Section~8.5). A good basis has vectors close in length to $\det(L)^{1/n}$ and close to orthogonal, which drives this ratio down toward its minimum possible value of $1$; a bad basis inflates the numerator $\prod_i\|b_i\|$ without changing $\det(L)$ at all, so the ratio grows the more skewed the basis is.
\end{previewbox}

\subsection{Gram--Schmidt Orthogonalization}

Given \emph{any} basis, can we systematically produce an orthogonal one (spanning the same space, though generally \emph{not} the same lattice --- more on that distinction below)? Yes --- and the entire idea rests on one simple geometric move: \textbf{taking a vector and subtracting off however much of it points along some other direction}, leaving only the part that's genuinely new. Let's make that move precise before writing down the full process.

\begin{defbox}{Vector Projection}
The \textbf{projection} of a vector $v$ onto a (nonzero) vector $u$ is
$$
\mathrm{proj}_u(v) \;=\; \frac{\langle v,u \rangle}{\langle u,u \rangle}\, u.
$$
Geometrically, $\mathrm{proj}_u(v)$ is the ``shadow'' $v$ casts on the line through $u$ --- the closest point to $v$ that lies on that line, i.e.\ the component of $v$ pointing in the $u$ direction. The leftover piece, $v - \mathrm{proj}_u(v)$, is exactly the component of $v$ perpendicular to $u$: check that $\langle v - \mathrm{proj}_u(v),\, u\rangle = \langle v,u\rangle - \frac{\langle v,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}\langle u,u\rangle = 0$, confirming orthogonality directly from the definition.
\end{defbox}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.15]
  \draw[-{Latex[length=2mm]}, thick, blue] (0,0) -- (2,0) node[below right, font=\small] {$b_1 = b_1^* = (2,0)$};
  \draw[-{Latex[length=2mm]}, thick, green!50!black] (0,0) -- (1,2) node[above left, font=\small] {$b_2 = (1,2)$};
  \draw[-{Latex[length=2mm]}, thick, orange, dashed] (0,0) -- (1,0);
  \node[orange, font=\scriptsize, below] at (0.5,-0.05) {$\mathrm{proj}_{b_1^*}(b_2) = (1,0)$};
  \draw[-{Latex[length=2mm]}, thick, red, dashed] (1,0) -- (1,2);
  \node[red, font=\small, right] at (1,1) {$b_2^* = (0,2)$};
  \draw (0.85,0) -- (0.85,0.15) -- (1,0.15);
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{The geometry behind one Gram--Schmidt step (numbers match the worked example below). The green vector $b_2$ decomposes into an orange piece --- its projection onto $b_1^*$, i.e.\ ``how much of $b_2$ points along $b_1^*$'' --- and a red perpendicular leftover, $b_2^* = b_2 - \mathrm{proj}_{b_1^*}(b_2)$. That red leftover, translated back to the origin, \emph{is} the new orthogonal basis vector. Every step of Gram--Schmidt is this same picture, repeated once per previous direction.}
\label{fig:gram-schmidt-projection}
\end{figure}

\begin{defbox}{Gram--Schmidt Process}
Given linearly independent $b_1,\ldots,b_k$, define $b_1^* = b_1$, and for $i = 2,\ldots,k$:
$$
b_i^* = b_i - \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \mu_{i,j}\, b_j^*, \qquad \text{where } \mu_{i,j} = \frac{\langle b_i, b_j^* \rangle}{\langle b_j^*, b_j^* \rangle}.
$$
Each term $\mu_{i,j}\,b_j^*$ is precisely $\mathrm{proj}_{b_j^*}(b_i)$ from the defbox above --- so this formula is nothing more than \textbf{``take $b_i$, and subtract off its projection onto every previously-built orthogonal direction, one at a time.''} Each $b_i^*$ is $b_i$ with the ``shadow'' it casts on all previous $b_j^*$ subtracted off, leaving only the genuinely new, orthogonal direction. The result $\{b_1^*,\ldots,b_k^*\}$ is an orthogonal basis of the same vector space (though, importantly, its integer combinations generally do \emph{not} produce the same lattice as the original $b_i$'s --- Gram--Schmidt vectors are a vector-space tool, not automatically a lattice basis).
\end{defbox}

\begin{exbox}{Gram--Schmidt on a $2 \times 2$ Example}
Let $b_1 = (2,0)$, $b_2 = (1,2)$ (the same vectors as Figure~\ref{fig:gram-schmidt-projection}). Then $b_1^* = b_1 = (2,0)$. Compute $\mu_{2,1} = \frac{\langle b_2,b_1^*\rangle}{\langle b_1^*,b_1^*\rangle} = \frac{(1)(2)+(2)(0)}{4} = \frac{2}{4} = 0.5$ --- this is exactly $\mathrm{proj}_{b_1^*}(b_2) = 0.5\,(2,0) = (1,0)$, the orange vector in the figure. So $b_2^* = b_2 - \mathrm{proj}_{b_1^*}(b_2) = (1,2) - (1,0) = (0,2)$ --- the red leftover. Check: $\langle b_1^*, b_2^* \rangle = (2)(0)+(0)(2) = 0$ --- orthogonal, as required.
\end{exbox}

\begin{previewbox}{Where Gram--Schmidt Leads}
This process reappears, in a modified ``integer-preserving'' form, inside the \textbf{LLL algorithm} --- the classic algorithm for turning a bad lattice basis into a reasonably good one, and a core tool used both to \emph{attack} weak lattice-based schemes and to reason about the security of well-parametrized ones. We'll meet it properly in a later lecture; for now, just remember that Gram--Schmidt is the geometric heart of it.
\end{previewbox}

\begin{exercisebox}{Practice}
Run Gram--Schmidt on $b_1 = (1,1)$, $b_2 = (0,2)$: compute $b_1^*$, $\mu_{2,1}$, and $b_2^*$, and verify $\langle b_1^*, b_2^* \rangle = 0$.
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Lattices: A Formal Definition}

Everything so far has allowed \emph{any} scalar from the field $F$ (e.g., any real number) when forming linear combinations. Informally, a lattice is what happens when we impose one dramatic restriction: \textbf{only integer combinations are allowed}, which turns a solid, continuous vector space into a discrete grid of isolated points. That informal picture (Figure~\ref{fig:latticegrid} below) is correct and worth keeping in your head. But the definition we will actually use --- the one used in serious treatments of the subject, such as Chris Peikert's graduate lecture notes \emph{Lattices in Cryptography}\footnote{C. Peikert, \emph{Lattices in Cryptography}, lecture notes, \url{https://github.com/cpeikert/LatticesInCryptography}.} --- starts from the \textbf{subgroup} idea from Section~3.1, not from a basis. We will show the two views agree.

\subsection{Discrete Sets}

\begin{defbox}{Discrete Subset}
A subset $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is \textbf{discrete} if every point of $S$ is \emph{isolated}: for every $x \in S$, there exists some $\varepsilon > 0$ such that the open ball $B(x,\varepsilon)$ (all points within distance $\varepsilon$ of $x$) contains \emph{no} point of $S$ other than $x$ itself. Informally: you can always draw a small enough circle around any point of $S$ that catches nothing else from $S$.
\end{defbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{A Shortcut for Subgroups}
Checking every single point of a set for isolation sounds like a lot of work. But if $H$ is a \emph{subgroup} of $(\mathbb{R}^n, +)$ (Section~3.1), there's a shortcut: it suffices to check isolation \emph{at the origin only} --- $H$ is discrete if and only if there is some $\varepsilon>0$ with $B(\vec{0},\varepsilon) \cap H = \{\vec{0}\}$. (This is a statement about how much \emph{checking} is needed, not about how many points end up isolated: as the argument below shows, once the origin passes this test, \emph{every} point of $H$ turns out to be isolated too --- not just the origin.) \emph{Why this works:} suppose $\vec 0$ is isolated with radius $\varepsilon$, and suppose some other point $y \in H$ were within $\varepsilon$ of some $x \in H$. Since $H$ is a subgroup, $y - x \in H$ as well, and $y - x$ is within $\varepsilon$ of $\vec 0$ --- so by our assumption, $y - x = \vec 0$, i.e., $y = x$. So checking one point (the origin) is enough to certify the whole subgroup is discrete, thanks to closure under subtraction.
\end{rmkbox}

\subsection{The Definition}

\begin{defbox}{Lattice}
A \textbf{lattice} is a discrete additive subgroup $L$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$. That is, $L \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a lattice if:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=(L\arabic*)]
  \item $L$ is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{R}^n, +)$ (Section~3.1): it contains $\vec 0$, and is closed under addition and negation.
  \item $L$ is discrete: by the shortcut above, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $B(\vec 0, \varepsilon) \cap L = \{\vec 0\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{defbox}

\begin{exbox}{$\mathbb{Q}$ Is a Subgroup, But \emph{Not} a Lattice}
Consider $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ (the rational numbers) under addition. This \emph{is} a subgroup: it contains $0$, the sum of two rationals is rational, and $-a$ is rational whenever $a$ is. So condition (L1) holds. But condition (L2) fails badly: the rationals are \textbf{dense} in $\mathbb{R}$, meaning every open ball around $0$, no matter how tiny, contains infinitely many other rational numbers. There is no $\varepsilon>0$ that isolates $0$. So $\mathbb{Q}$ is a perfectly good subgroup that is \textbf{not} a lattice --- this is exactly why condition (L2) has to be part of the definition; subgroup alone is not a strong enough requirement.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}{$\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}^n$ Are Lattices}
$\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is a subgroup (trivially --- and recall from Section~3.1 that $n\mathbb{Z}$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ for any $n$; here we're just looking at $\mathbb{Z}$ itself sitting inside $\mathbb{R}$), and it is discrete: taking $\varepsilon = 0.5$, $B(0, 0.5) \cap \mathbb{Z} = \{0\}$, since the nearest other integers ($\pm 1$) are a full unit away. So $\mathbb{Z}$ is a lattice in $\mathbb{R}$ --- the simplest possible example. The same argument, coordinate by coordinate, shows $\mathbb{Z}^n \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ (all integer-coordinate points) is a lattice in $\mathbb{R}^n$; it is often called \textbf{the integer lattice}.
\end{exbox}

\subsection{The Shortest Vector and the Minimum Distance $\lambda_1(L)$}

Before going further, it's worth naming one basic quantity attached to every lattice: how close together its points actually are. We will use this constantly for the rest of the lecture (and the rest of the course), so it earns a formal definition rather than staying an informal aside.

\begin{defbox}{Shortest Vector and Minimum Distance $\lambda_1(L)$}
Let $L \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a lattice. A \textbf{shortest vector} of $L$ is a nonzero vector $v \in L$ whose norm is minimal among all nonzero lattice vectors. The length of a shortest vector is called the lattice's \textbf{minimum distance}, or \textbf{first successive minimum}, and is defined by
\[
\lambda_1(L)
\;=\;
\min_{v \in L \setminus \{\vec{0}\}} \|v\|.
\]

The subscript ``$1$'' is intentional. More generally, the \textbf{successive minima}
\[
\lambda_1(L) \leq \lambda_2(L) \leq \cdots \leq \lambda_n(L)
\]
describe the minimum lengths needed to obtain $1,2,\ldots,n$ linearly independent lattice vectors, respectively. In particular, $\lambda_i(L)$ is the smallest radius $r$ such that the ball
\[
B_n(\vec{0},r)
=
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n : \|x\|\leq r\}
\]
contains $i$ linearly independent vectors of $L$.

Thus, $\lambda_1(L)$ is simply the length of a shortest nonzero lattice vector. In this course, we will only need $\lambda_1(L)$ until the optional \textbf{Minkowski's Second Theorem} box near the end of this section.
\end{defbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Why the Minimum Is Actually Attained (Not Just an Infimum)}
For a general infinite set, ``the smallest nonzero norm'' might not literally be achieved by any single element (imagine values getting closer and closer to some limit without ever reaching it). This cannot happen for a lattice, precisely \emph{because} $L$ is discrete (Section~8.1): discreteness guarantees a gap $\varepsilon>0$ around the origin with no other lattice points inside it, so only \emph{finitely many} lattice points can lie in any bounded region --- and the minimum of a norm over a finite set is always achieved. This is also exactly the first step of the ``harder direction'' proof sketch below (Section~8.4): $\lambda_1(L)$ isn't just a number we can talk about, it's a specific vector we can (in principle) point to.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Why $\lambda_1(L)$ Matters}
Computing $\lambda_1(L)$ exactly --- or even just estimating it well --- for a lattice given only a (possibly bad) basis is called the \textbf{Shortest Vector Problem (SVP)}, and it is believed to be extremely hard once the dimension $n$ is large. This hardness is one of the two or three core hard problems that lattice-based cryptography's security ultimately rests on; we will state SVP formally, and meet its hardness in practice, once we reach the LWE problem in the next lecture.
\end{rmkbox}

\subsection{Recovering the Basis Picture}

The subgroup definition is more general and more standard, but the ``grid generated by a basis'' picture you may already have in your head is not wrong --- it is a theorem, not a definition, and it's worth seeing why.

\begin{factbox}{Basis Representation of Lattices}
Let $L \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Then $L$ is a lattice (in the sense of the subgroup definition above, i.e., a discrete additive subgroup) \textbf{if and only if} there exist linearly independent vectors $b_1, \ldots, b_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (with $k \le n$) such that
$$
L = \mathcal{L}(b_1,\ldots,b_k) = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i b_i \;\middle|\; a_i \in \mathbb{Z} \right\}.
$$
The vectors $b_1,\ldots,b_k$ are called a \textbf{basis} of $L$; the number $k$ is the \textbf{rank} of $L$. If $k=n$ (the basis spans all of $\mathbb{R}^n$), $L$ is called \textbf{full-rank} --- this is the common case in cryptography.
\end{factbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Proof Idea (Sketch Only)}
\textbf{Easy direction ($\Leftarrow$):} given linearly independent $b_1,\ldots,b_k$, the set of integer combinations is clearly a subgroup (adding two integer combinations, or negating one, gives another integer combination). Discreteness holds because the $b_i$ point in genuinely independent directions: you cannot make $\sum a_i b_i$ arbitrarily close to $\vec 0$ without every $a_i$ being exactly $0$ --- linear independence rules out any ``near-cancellation.'' (Making this fully rigorous uses the fact that the linear map $(a_1,\ldots,a_k) \mapsto \sum a_i b_i$ has a bounded inverse on its image.)

\textbf{Harder direction ($\Rightarrow$):} given only that $L$ is a discrete subgroup, we must \emph{construct} a basis. The idea is greedy and inductive: pick $b_1$ to be a shortest nonzero vector in $L$ --- exactly $\lambda_1(L)$ from the defbox above, which exists precisely \emph{because} $L$ is discrete (in a non-discrete set like $\mathbb{Q}$, there is no shortest nonzero element, since you can always find a smaller one). Then look at the part of $L$ not lying on the line through $b_1$, and pick $b_2$ to be a shortest vector there, and so on. A careful argument (using discreteness at each step, and projecting onto the space orthogonal to the vectors already chosen) shows this process terminates with a valid basis after $k \le n$ steps. The full details are carried out rigorously in Peikert's notes; we take the result as given here.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Same Lattice, Different Basis}
A single lattice has \emph{many} different valid bases (just as a vector space does --- recall the ``Basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$'' example in Section~7). Two bases generate the exact same set of points if and only if one can be converted to the other using an integer matrix with determinant $\pm 1$ (a \textbf{unimodular} transformation) --- we won't need the details of this fact yet, but the consequence is critical: some bases consist of short, nearly-perpendicular vectors (a ``good'' basis, easy to compute with), while other, equally valid bases for the \emph{same} lattice consist of long, nearly-parallel vectors (a ``bad'' basis, hard to compute with). \textbf{This gap between good and bad bases for the same lattice is the single most important idea in lattice-based cryptography}: the secret key is typically a good (short) basis, while the public key describes the same lattice using a bad (long, scrambled-looking) basis. Recovering the good basis from the bad one is believed to be extremely hard once the dimension $n$ is large enough.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{exbox}{A Tiny Lattice by Hand}
Let $b_1 = (2,0)$ and $b_2 = (0,3)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The lattice $\mathcal{L}(b_1,b_2)$ consists of every point $(2a_1, 3a_2)$ for integers $a_1, a_2$ --- so it includes $(0,0)$, $(2,0)$, $(-2,0)$, $(0,3)$, $(2,3)$, $(4,-3)$, and so on, but \emph{not} $(1,1)$ or $(1,0)$, since those cannot be written as $(2a_1, 3a_2)$ for integer $a_1, a_2$. As a sanity check against the subgroup definition directly: is this set a subgroup? Yes --- closed under addition and negation, contains $(0,0)$. Is it discrete? Yes --- the nearest point to the origin is at distance $2$, so any $\varepsilon < 2$ isolates $\vec 0$. (Equivalently: $\lambda_1(L) = 2$, achieved by $b_1$ itself.)
\end{exbox}

\subsection{The Determinant (Volume) of a Lattice}
One more piece of structure will matter a great deal in later lectures:
every full-rank lattice has a well-defined ``volume,'' independent of
which basis is used to describe it.
\begin{defbox}{Fundamental Domain and Determinant}
Let $L \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a full-rank lattice with basis
$b_1,\ldots,b_n$, and let $B$ be the matrix whose columns are
$b_1,\ldots,b_n$. The \textbf{fundamental parallelepiped} associated
with this basis is
\[
\mathcal{P}(B)
=
\left\{
\sum_{i=1}^n t_i b_i
\;\middle|\;
0 \leq t_i < 1
\right\}.
\]
Here, each coefficient $t_i$ can be \emph{any real number} in the
interval $[0,1)$. Thus, $\mathcal{P}(B)$ consists of all fractional
linear combinations of the basis vectors, not merely combinations
using coefficients $0$ or $1$.
The \textbf{determinant} of the lattice $L$, denoted by $\det(L)$, is
the volume of its fundamental parallelepiped:
\[
\det(L)
=
\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{P}(B))
=
|\det(B)|.
\]
\end{defbox}
The fundamental parallelepiped can be viewed as a basic ``tile'' for
the entire space. Copies of $\mathcal{P}(B)$, shifted by every lattice
point $\ell\in L$, tile all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ \emph{exactly}: every
point of $\mathbb{R}^n$ lies in \emph{one and only one} translated
copy, with no overlap at all. (This is precisely why the definition
uses the half-open condition $0 \le t_i < 1$ rather than
$0 \le t_i \le 1$: it guarantees each point has a unique
representative $p \in \mathcal{P}(B)$, avoiding double-counting along
shared faces.) Formally,
\[
\mathbb{R}^n
=
\bigsqcup_{\ell\in L}
\left(\ell+\mathcal{P}(B)\right),
\]
where $\bigsqcup$ denotes a disjoint union.
Here, \textbf{shifting} (or \textbf{translating}) $\mathcal{P}(B)$ by
a lattice point $\ell$ simply means adding $\ell$ to every point in
the parallelepiped:
\[
\ell+\mathcal{P}(B)
=
\left\{
\ell+x
\;\middle|\;
x\in\mathcal{P}(B)
\right\}.
\]
The shape and volume do not change; only the location changes.
\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Example: the lattice $\mathbb{Z}^2$.}
Consider the lattice
\[
L=\mathbb{Z}^2
\]
with basis
\[
b_1=(1,0),
\qquad
b_2=(0,1).
\]
According to the definition,
\[
\mathcal{P}(B)
=
\left\{
t_1b_1+t_2b_2
\;\middle|\;
0\leq t_1,t_2<1
\right\}.
\]
Substituting the basis vectors gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
t_1b_1+t_2b_2
&=
t_1(1,0)+t_2(0,1)\\
&=
(t_1,0)+(0,t_2)\\
&=
(t_1,t_2).
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore,
\[
\mathcal{P}(B)
=
\left\{
(t_1,t_2)
\;\middle|\;
0\leq t_1,t_2<1
\right\}
=
[0,1)\times[0,1).
\]
Notice that $t_1$ and $t_2$ are not restricted to the values $0$ and
$1$. They can take any real values between $0$ and $1$. For example,
\[
0.3b_1+0.7b_2
=
(0.3,0.7)
\in\mathcal{P}(B).
\]
Thus, $\mathcal{P}(B)$ contains every point in the unit square
$[0,1)\times[0,1)$.
Now consider the lattice point
\[
\ell=(2,1).
\]
Shifting the fundamental parallelepiped by $\ell$ gives
\[
\ell+\mathcal{P}(B)
=
[2,3)\times[1,2).
\]
For example,
\[
(0,0)\mapsto(2,1),
\qquad
(0.3,0.7)\mapsto(2.3,1.7).
\]
Thus, shifting by $(2,1)$ simply moves the entire unit square two
units to the right and one unit upward. Notice that
$\mathcal{P}(B)=[0,1)\times[0,1)$ and
$\ell+\mathcal{P}(B)=[2,3)\times[1,2)$ are entirely different sets of
points: they do not share any point in common, illustrating the
disjointness of the tiling above.
More generally, every point $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$ can be written
\emph{uniquely} as
\[
x=\ell+p,
\qquad
\ell\in L,\quad p\in\mathcal{P}(B).
\]
The lattice point $\ell$ determines the translated copy of the
fundamental parallelepiped, while $p$ determines the position of $x$
within that copy.
Since translation does not change volume, every translated copy
$\ell+\mathcal{P}(B)$ has the same volume as $\mathcal{P}(B)$. Thus,
the determinant of the lattice can be interpreted geometrically as
the volume of one fundamental ``cell'' of the lattice:
\[
\boxed{\det(L)=|\det(B)|.}
\]

\begin{rmkbox}{Why $\det(L)$ Doesn't Depend on Which Basis You Pick}
Recall from the ``Same Lattice, Different Basis'' remark above: any two bases of the same lattice are related by a unimodular matrix $U$ (integer entries, $\det(U) = \pm 1$), i.e., $B' = UB$. Then $\det(B') = \det(U)\det(B) = \pm \det(B)$, so $|\det(B')| = |\det(B)|$. This confirms $\det(L)$ is a genuine property of the lattice itself, not an artifact of which basis happened to be used to compute it --- exactly the kind of basis-independence you should expect from something called ``the volume of the lattice.''
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{exbox}{Computing $\det(L)$ for the Tiny Lattice}
For $b_1=(2,0)$, $b_2=(0,3)$ from the example above, $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\det(L) = |\det(B)| = |2 \times 3 - 0 \times 0| = 6$. Geometrically: the fundamental parallelepiped is just the $2\times 3$ rectangle $[0,2)\times[0,3)$, and copies of this rectangle exactly tile the plane, anchored at every lattice point --- matching the picture in Figure~\ref{fig:latticegrid}'s style, just for this specific (axis-aligned) lattice.
\end{exbox}

\subsection{Unimodular Equivalence: When Do Two Bases Give the Same Lattice?}

\begin{exbox}{Same Lattice, or Different? A Worked Test Using the Unimodular Criterion}
Let $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (columns $b_1=(2,1), b_2=(1,1)$) and $B' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (columns $b_1'=(1,0), b_2'=(3,1)$) be two candidate bases in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Do they generate the same lattice?

\textbf{Confirming case.} Suppose instead $B' = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Since $B$ is invertible ($\det B = 2-1=1 \ne 0$), solve $U = B^{-1}B'$: $B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ (check: $B B^{-1}=I$). Then
$$
U = B^{-1}B' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
$U$ has integer entries and $\det(U) = 1\times1 - 0\times1 = 1$, so $|\det(U)|=1$ --- $U$ \textbf{is unimodular}. Since $B' = BU$ with $U$ unimodular, $B$ and $B'$ generate \textbf{the same lattice}.

\textbf{Contrasting case.} Now go back to the original $B' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Compute $U = B^{-1}B'$:
$$
U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
Still integer entries, but $\det(U) = (1)(-1) - (2)(-1) = -1+2 = 1$ --- wait, this is also unimodular; let's instead deliberately pick a genuinely different lattice to illustrate the failing case: $B'' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Then
$$
U = B^{-1}B'' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
$$
with $\det(U) = (1)(1)-(1)(-1) = 2$. Since $|\det(U)| = 2 \ne 1$, $U$ is \textbf{not} unimodular --- $B$ and $B''$ generate \emph{different} lattices, and in fact $\mathcal{L}(B'')$ turns out to be a proper sublattice of $\mathcal{L}(B)$ of \textbf{index $2$} (every point of $\mathcal{L}(B'')$ is a point of $\mathcal{L}(B)$, but only half of $\mathcal{L}(B)$'s points are recovered this way) --- a direct consequence of $|\det(U)|$ measuring exactly this kind of ``index'' between the two lattices, matching $\det(L) = |\det(B)|$ scaling by the same factor: $\det(\mathcal{L}(B'')) = |\det(U)|\cdot\det(\mathcal{L}(B)) = 2\det(\mathcal{L}(B))$.
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Discrete Implies Countable, But Not Conversely}
It's worth clarifying a subtle point about the discreteness condition (L2) in the Lattice definition: \textbf{every discrete subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is automatically countable}, but the converse is false --- being countable does not force a set to be discrete. \emph{Why discrete $\Rightarrow$ countable:} around each point of a discrete set, by definition, sits a small ball containing no other point of the set; these disjoint balls can be shrunk enough to have rational-coordinate centers and rational radii, and there are only countably many such balls in total (since $\mathbb{Q}^n$ is countable) --- so there can be at most countably many points of the discrete set, one per ball. \emph{Why the converse fails:} $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is a textbook counterexample --- it is countable (the rationals can be listed $q_1, q_2, q_3,\ldots$), yet, as the exbox above on ``$\mathbb{Q}$ Is a Subgroup, But Not a Lattice'' already showed, $\mathbb{Q}$ is \emph{dense} in $\mathbb{R}$, not discrete: every open ball around any point contains infinitely many other rational points. So countability is a necessary, but nowhere near sufficient, condition for discreteness --- exactly why the Lattice definition needs the stronger, explicitly geometric condition (L2), rather than settling for the weaker ``countable'' in its place.
\end{rmkbox}

\subsection{Minkowski's First Theorem: Volume Forces a Short Vector to Exist}

We now have the two basic numbers attached to any lattice: $\lambda_1(L)$ (how short its shortest vector is) and $\det(L)$ (how much volume, on average, surrounds each lattice point). Minkowski's First Theorem is the single most important fact connecting them --- and it flips the usual question around. So far, ``finding $\lambda_1(L)$'' has meant: given a specific basis, go and search for a short vector. Minkowski's theorem asks instead: \emph{without searching at all}, can we predict, from $\det(L)$ alone, an upper bound on how long $\lambda_1(L)$ is \emph{allowed} to be? Surprisingly, yes --- and, remarkably, the argument is pure geometry, with no algebra and no knowledge of any particular basis required.

The theorem is about regions $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ with two specific shape properties, \textbf{symmetric} and \textbf{convex}, both used informally already in this course but worth pinning down precisely before they appear inside a theorem statement.

\begin{defbox}{Symmetric Region (Origin-Symmetric / Centrally Symmetric)}
A region $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is \textbf{symmetric} (short for \emph{origin-symmetric}, sometimes called \emph{centrally symmetric} or \emph{point-symmetric}) if
$$
K = -K, \qquad \text{i.e.,} \qquad x \in K \ \Longrightarrow\ -x \in K.
$$
In words: whenever a point $x$ belongs to $K$, its reflection through the origin, $-x$, must belong to $K$ too. Informally, $K$ ``looks the same'' if you rotate it $180^\circ$ about the origin --- a disk or square \emph{centered at the origin} is symmetric in this sense, but the same disk or square shifted off-center is not (its reflection through the origin lands somewhere else entirely).
\end{defbox}

\begin{defbox}{Convex Region}
A region $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is \textbf{convex} if, for every pair of points $x, y \in K$, the entire straight line segment joining them,
$$
\{\, t x + (1-t) y \;:\; t \in [0,1] \,\},
$$
also lies inside $K$. Informally: a convex region has no ``dents,'' ``notches,'' or ``holes'' --- you can never draw a straight line between two points of $K$ that has to leave $K$ and come back. Disks, squares, triangles, and ellipses are all convex; rings (annuli), crescents, and star shapes generally are not.
\end{defbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{The Two Properties Are Independent}
Symmetric and convex describe two \emph{different} things about a region's shape --- one is about ``does it look the same reflected through the origin,'' the other is about ``does it have any dents or holes'' --- so a region can have either property without the other, both, or neither. Minkowski's theorem needs \emph{both at once}: Figure~\ref{fig:sym-convex-examples} below shows one example of each of the four possible combinations.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{The Idea in One Sentence, Before Any Symbols}
If a symmetric, convex region around the origin is made large enough relative to $\det(L)$, it becomes \emph{geometrically impossible} for that region to avoid every nonzero lattice point --- so one must be hiding inside it, whether or not you know where.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{defbox}{Minkowski's First Theorem}
Let $L \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a full-rank lattice, and let $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a \textbf{symmetric} ($K = -K$, i.e.\ $x \in K \Rightarrow -x \in K$) \textbf{convex} region. If
$$
\mathrm{Vol}(K) \;>\; 2^n \det(L),
$$
then $K$ contains a nonzero lattice point, i.e.\ $K \cap (L \setminus \{\vec 0\}) \ne \varnothing$.
\end{defbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Reading the Theorem in Plain Language, Term by Term}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{$\det(L)$}, as in Section~8.5, is ``how much empty space, on average, surrounds each lattice point.''
  \item \textbf{$2^n \det(L)$} is a fixed volume threshold, scaled up from $\det(L)$ by a factor that grows with the dimension $n$ (we will see exactly where this factor comes from in the proof below).
  \item \textbf{Symmetric and convex} are conditions on the \emph{shape} of $K$ --- a disk or an ellipsoid centered at the origin both qualify; an oddly-shaped or off-center region generally does not.
  \item \textbf{The conclusion} is purely existential: once $K$'s volume clears the threshold, $K$ is \emph{guaranteed} to contain some nonzero point of $L$ --- but the theorem does not say which point, or how to find it. That ``existence without construction'' flavor is typical of the deep facts underlying lattice cryptography, and it's worth getting comfortable with that style of guarantee now.
\end{itemize}
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{A Loose End First: Does $K$ Always Contain the Origin?}
Before turning to the proof, it's worth settling a question the theorem's statement doesn't address directly: could a symmetric convex region fail to contain the origin at all? No --- and this is worth proving explicitly, because it removes a genuine ambiguity later.

\emph{Claim: any nonempty symmetric convex set $K$ contains $\vec 0$.} \emph{Proof:} since $K$ is nonempty, pick any point $x \in K$. By symmetry ($K=-K$), $-x \in K$ too. By convexity, the line segment joining $x$ and $-x$ lies entirely in $K$ --- in particular its midpoint, $\tfrac{1}{2}(x + (-x)) = \vec 0$, is in $K$. $\blacksquare$

So $\vec 0 \in K$ is automatic, for \emph{every} symmetric convex $K$, regardless of its size --- it has nothing to do with the volume threshold $2^n\det(L)$ in the theorem. The origin is never at risk of being excluded, so the theorem's volume condition can't be about keeping the origin in; it must be doing something else. That something else is the theorem's real content: guaranteeing a \emph{second}, \emph{nonzero} lattice point, which is not automatic, and genuinely does depend on $K$ being large enough.
\end{rmkbox}

\subsubsection{A Fully Formal Proof, via Blichfeldt's Lemma}

\begin{rmkbox}{Proof Roadmap}
The proof splits cleanly into two independent pieces, and it helps to see the two-step strategy before working through either one in detail:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Blichfeldt's Lemma} (a pure volume/pigeonhole argument): \emph{any} region with volume bigger than $\det(L)$ --- symmetric, convex, or neither, it doesn't matter --- must contain two distinct points whose difference is a nonzero lattice vector. This step never looks at the shape of the region at all.
  \item \textbf{Bringing back symmetry and convexity}: apply Blichfeldt's Lemma not to $K$ itself, but to the \emph{half-sized} copy $S = \tfrac12 K$. The difference of the two points Blichfeldt hands us then turns out, \emph{because} $K$ is symmetric and convex, to land back inside $K$ itself --- which is exactly the nonzero lattice point in $K$ that the theorem promises. This is also where the factor of $2^n$ in the theorem's statement comes from: shrinking $K$ down to $S=\tfrac12K$ divides its volume by $2^n$, so $S$ only clears Blichfeldt's threshold ($\mathrm{Vol}(S) > \det(L)$) once $\mathrm{Vol}(K) > 2^n\det(L)$ to begin with.
\end{enumerate}
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{factbox}{Blichfeldt's Lemma}
Let $L \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a full-rank lattice with fundamental domain $\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{P}(B)$ (Section~8.5), so $\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{P}) = \det(L)$. Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be any measurable set with $\mathrm{Vol}(S) > \det(L)$. Then there exist two \textbf{distinct} points $p, q \in S$ such that $p - q \in L \setminus \{\vec 0\}$.
\end{factbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Proof of Blichfeldt's Lemma}
\textbf{Step 1: Cut $S$ into pieces, one per lattice point, and fold every piece back to the origin.} For each lattice point $v \in L$, consider the piece of $S$ that lands in the copy of the fundamental domain sitting at $v$, i.e.\ $S \cap (v + \mathcal{P})$, and \textbf{fold it back} to the origin's copy by subtracting $v$:
$$
S_v \;=\; \big(S \cap (v+\mathcal{P})\big) - v \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{P}.
$$

\textbf{Step 2: The folded pieces' volumes must add up to more than $\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{P})$.} Because the translates $\{v + \mathcal{P} : v \in L\}$ tile $\mathbb{R}^n$ with no gaps or overlaps (Section~8.5), every point of $S$ lies in \emph{exactly one} $v + \mathcal{P}$, so the sets $S \cap (v+\mathcal{P})$, as $v$ ranges over $L$, partition $S$ exactly. Folding back (a rigid shift) doesn't change volume, so
$$
\sum_{v \in L} \mathrm{Vol}(S_v) \;=\; \sum_{v\in L} \mathrm{Vol}\big(S \cap (v+\mathcal{P})\big) \;=\; \mathrm{Vol}(S) \;>\; \det(L) \;=\; \mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{P}).
$$

\textbf{Step 3: Too much volume packed into one region forces an overlap (pigeonhole).} All the $S_v$'s live inside the single region $\mathcal{P}$. If they were all pairwise disjoint, the sum of their volumes could be at most $\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{P})$ --- but Step 2 just showed that sum is \emph{strictly greater} than $\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{P})$. Contradiction. So some two of them, $S_{v_1}$ and $S_{v_2}$ with $v_1 \ne v_2$, must overlap: there is a point $y \in S_{v_1} \cap S_{v_2}$.

\textbf{Step 4: Unfold the overlap to recover two points of $S$ whose difference is a lattice vector.} By definition of $S_v$, $y = p - v_1$ for some $p \in S \cap (v_1+\mathcal{P})$, and $y = q - v_2$ for some $q \in S \cap (v_2+\mathcal{P})$. Both $p,q \in S$. Since $p - v_1 = q - v_2$, we get
$$
p - q \;=\; v_1 - v_2 \;\in\; L\setminus\{\vec 0\}
$$
(nonzero because $v_1 \ne v_2$, and the difference of two lattice points is again a lattice point, by $L$'s subgroup closure from Section~3.1). This is exactly the pair the lemma promised. $\blacksquare$
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{exbox}{Blichfeldt's Lemma, Checked by Hand}
Take $L = \mathbb{Z}^2$ ($\det(L)=1$) and $S = [0,1.5]\times[0,1]$, area $1.5 > 1$. Following the proof: the piece of $S$ in the fundamental domain $\mathcal{P}=[0,1)\times[0,1)$ itself is all of $[0,1)\times[0,1)$ (area $1$); the piece of $S$ in the neighboring copy $v+\mathcal{P}$ for $v=(1,0)$ is $[1,1.5]\times[0,1)$ (area $0.5$), which folds back to $[0,0.5)\times[0,1)$ after subtracting $v$. These two folded pieces --- area $1$ and area $0.5$, total $1.5 > 1 = \mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{P})$ --- cannot both fit disjointly inside $\mathcal{P}$, so they overlap on $[0,0.5)\times[0,1)$. Picking any point there, e.g.\ $(0.25,0.5)$: this equals $p - (0,0) = p$ for $p=(0.25,0.5) \in S$, and also equals $q - (1,0)$ for $q = (1.25,0.5) \in S$. Indeed $p,q \in S$ (check: $0.25, 1.25 \in [0,1.5]$, $0.5\in[0,1]$, both confirmed), and $p - q = (-1,0) \in \mathbb{Z}^2\setminus\{\vec 0\}$ --- exactly as guaranteed, and matching Figure~\ref{fig:blichfeldt}.
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Deriving Minkowski's Theorem from Blichfeldt's Lemma}
Now bring back symmetry and convexity to finish the proof, following Step~2 of the roadmap above. Let $K$ be symmetric and convex with $\mathrm{Vol}(K) > 2^n\det(L)$.

\textbf{Set up the shrunk region.} Define $S = \tfrac{1}{2}K = \{\tfrac12 x : x \in K\}$. Volume scales with the $n$-th power of a linear scale factor, so $\mathrm{Vol}(S) = \mathrm{Vol}(K)/2^n > \det(L)$ --- exactly the hypothesis Blichfeldt's Lemma needs.

\textbf{Apply Blichfeldt's Lemma to $S$.} There exist distinct $p, q \in S$ with $p - q \in L\setminus\{\vec 0\}$. Since $p,q \in S = \tfrac12 K$, write $p = \tfrac12 x$ and $q = \tfrac12 y$ for (unique) $x, y \in K$. Then
$$
p - q \;=\; \tfrac12(x - y) \;=\; \tfrac12\big(x + (-y)\big).
$$

\textbf{Show this difference lands back inside $K$.} Since $K$ is symmetric, $-y \in K$. Since $K$ is convex, the midpoint of $x$ and $-y$ --- which is exactly $\tfrac12(x+(-y)) = p-q$ --- lies in $K$. So $p - q$ is simultaneously:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item a nonzero lattice vector (from Blichfeldt's Lemma), and
  \item a point of $K$ (from symmetry and convexity, just shown).
\end{itemize}
Therefore $p - q \in K \cap (L\setminus\{\vec 0\})$ --- a nonzero lattice point inside $K$, exactly as the theorem claims. $\blacksquare$
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{Tying the Two Halves Together}
Notice how cleanly the labor divides. Blichfeldt's Lemma is the \emph{engine}: pure volume-counting, no geometry of $K$'s shape involved at all --- it would work verbatim for \emph{any} measurable $S$, symmetric or not, convex or not, as long as it's simply big enough. Symmetry and convexity only enter in the very last step, to upgrade the raw difference $p-q$ (which Blichfeldt hands you for free) into a point that's \emph{also} guaranteed to lie inside $K$ itself, not just somewhere in $\mathbb{R}^n$. This is worth remembering: it's the reason Minkowski's theorem needs \emph{both} hypotheses (symmetric \emph{and} convex) and not just ``big enough'' --- drop either one, and the last step's midpoint trick breaks, even though Blichfeldt's Lemma itself still holds regardless.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{exbox}{Minkowski's Bound on the Simplest Lattice, $L = \mathbb{Z}^2$}
Take $L = \mathbb{Z}^2$, so $\det(L) = 1$ (Section~8.5). Let $K$ be a disk of radius $r$ centered at the origin --- symmetric and convex, exactly as the theorem requires. The theorem applies once $\mathrm{Vol}(K) = \pi r^2 > 2^2 \cdot \det(L) = 4$, i.e.\ once
$$
r > \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \approx 1.128.
$$
So Minkowski's theorem \emph{guarantees}, without any further work, that a disk of radius $1.13$ around the origin must contain a nonzero lattice point. Checking directly: we already know $\lambda_1(\mathbb{Z}^2) = 1$ (the point $(1,0)$), and indeed $1 < 1.128$ --- consistent with, and in fact comfortably inside, the guarantee. Notice the theorem didn't need to know that $(1,0)$ was the answer; it only needed the disk to be big enough.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}{The Same Bound on a Very Different-Looking Lattice}
Now take a stretched lattice with basis $b_1 = (5,0)$, $b_2 = (0,1)$, so $\det(L) = |5 \times 1 - 0 \times 0| = 5$ (Section~8.5). The disk threshold becomes $\pi r^2 > 4 \times 5 = 20$, i.e.\ $r > \sqrt{20/\pi} \approx 2.523$. Minkowski's theorem guarantees a nonzero lattice point within distance $2.523$ of the origin. The actual shortest vector here is $(0,1)$, of length $1$ --- again comfortably inside the guaranteed radius, but this time the guarantee is quite \emph{loose}: the theorem promised ``within $2.523$'' and reality delivered ``within $1$.'' This is expected and normal --- Minkowski's theorem is a \textbf{worst-case guarantee}, not an exact formula; it never overpromises (the bound always holds), but it can certainly underdeliver on precision for any one specific lattice. Its real value is as a \emph{universal} ceiling that holds for \emph{every} lattice of a given determinant, not as a precise estimate for one.
\end{exbox}

\begin{rmkbox}{From the Disk Bound to the General Formula}
Redo the disk calculation above in $n$ dimensions instead of $2$: the volume of a radius-$r$ ball in $\mathbb{R}^n$ shrinks (relative to $r^n$) as $n$ grows, and pushing the same threshold argument through (we omit the $n$-dimensional volume formula itself, which involves the Gamma function) yields the general bound already stated in Lecture 1's introduction to this topic:
$$
\lambda_1(L) \;\le\; \sqrt{n}\cdot \det(L)^{1/n} \qquad \text{(up to a small constant factor).}
$$
\textbf{In plain terms:} a lattice can never ``hide'' an unexpectedly long shortest vector behind a small determinant --- volume and minimum distance are locked together by geometry alone, regardless of which basis you're handed to describe the lattice. This is part of \emph{why} SVP is only believed hard in the specific regime cryptographers deliberately choose (large dimension $n$, carefully scaled $\det(L)$), rather than for arbitrary lattices --- a preview of the parameter-selection discussion we'll return to once we look at how Kyber and Dilithium choose their concrete dimensions and moduli.
\end{rmkbox}

\begin{previewbox}{Optional / Advanced: Minkowski's Second Theorem}
The First Theorem only talks about $\lambda_1(L)$, the \emph{single} shortest vector. It says nothing about the second-shortest independent direction, the third, and so on. \textbf{Minkowski's Second Theorem} fills that gap: recall the \textbf{successive minima} $\lambda_1(L) \le \lambda_2(L) \le \cdots \le \lambda_n(L)$ flagged in the defbox above, where $\lambda_i(L)$ is the smallest radius $r$ such that the ball of radius $r$ contains $i$ \emph{linearly independent} lattice vectors. The theorem bounds their \emph{product}:
$$
\frac{2^n}{n!}\det(L) \;\le\; \lambda_1(L)\cdots\lambda_n(L) \;\le\; 2^n \det(L).
$$
This is not core material --- everything in Sessions 1--6 (understanding LWE, the algorithms, and the physical attacks in Section 2 of the proposal) only ever leans on the First Theorem's intuition: ``small determinant $\Rightarrow$ short vector must exist.'' The Second Theorem becomes genuinely useful only once the research moves toward analyzing \emph{trapdoor} lattices and Gaussian sampling in depth --- which is exactly what underlies Falcon's signing procedure (flagged for Session 5). A trapdoor basis needs \emph{all} of its vectors short, not just the shortest one, for Gaussian sampling to be both efficient and secure; that ``all vectors short together'' requirement is precisely what the successive minima $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ capture, and the Second Theorem is the tool that ties them back to $\det(L)$. This box is worth returning to \emph{if and only if} the Phase 2 research direction (Section~4 of the proposal) ends up involving Falcon's sampler or trapdoor generation in detail --- otherwise, the First Theorem alone is sufficient background.
\end{previewbox}
